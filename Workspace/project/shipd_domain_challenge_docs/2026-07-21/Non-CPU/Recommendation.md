> Biocatalytic Product Recommendation: Ranking Candidate Products by Enzyme-Reaction Relevance
> Overview
> You are building a learning-to-rank / recommendation model for enzymatic chemistry. Each record pairs
> an enzyme-reaction context — a set of substrate molecules together with the catalyzing enzyme class
> (its EC number) — with a candidate product molecule:
> substrates: CCCCCCCCCCCCCCCCCCO.NC(=O)c1ccc[n+](...NAD+ cofactor...)c1 ec: 1.1.1.71 candidate: CCCCCCCCCCCCCCCCC=O
> For each record you output a single relevance score in [0, 1]: how strongly this enzyme-context
> supports candidate as its reaction product. Products the enzyme actually makes should be ranked at the
> top (high relevance); products it does not make should sink to the bottom (low relevance). Here the
> candidate is the context's real product (an alcohol dehydrogenase, EC 1.1.1.x, oxidizes the primary alcohol
> of the first substrate to an aldehyde; the second substrate is the NAD+ cofactor), so it should rank high.
> No molecule is generated — you rank/score candidates, you do not produce them.
> The training data carries implicit relevance feedback: each enzyme-context appears paired with its
> observed product (feedback 1) and with sampled negatives (feedback 0) — candidate products that
> this specific enzyme-context does not make. You learn a ranker from this feedback and apply it to enzyme
> contexts and products you have not seen.
> The ec field is the four-level EC number (Enzyme Commission number, e.g. 1.1.1.71); its leading
> digit is the top-level enzyme class (1 oxidoreductases, 2 transferases, 3 hydrolases, 4 lyases,
> 5 isomerases, 6 ligases, 7 translocases). The substrates field is a single dot-separated SMILES string
> and includes any cofactors (NAD(P)H, ATP, coenzyme A, ...) the enzyme uses.
> The sampled negatives are engineered to be informative: many are hard negatives — an enzyme's real
> product paired with the wrong enzyme class from the same top-level family (same EC first digit) — so the
> candidate is a chemically plausible product for that kind of enzyme and surface similarity between
> candidate and substrates is uninformative; only modelling the specific enzyme's activity ranks them
> correctly. A good ranker both orders observed products above negatives and reports well-calibrated
> relevance. The intended apparatus is a trained learning-to-rank / recommendation model — for example a
> fine-tuned chemical language model used as a cross-encoder, a graph neural network, or a gradient-boosted
> model over reaction-difference features.
> Data
> All files live under public/ and share a single identifier column, id (present in train.csv,
> test.csv, sample_submission.csv, and your submission). The dataset contains about 18,000 records in
> total, split into 14,714 training records (train.csv) and 3,286 test records (test.csv); each
> split is an even mix of observed products and sampled negatives.
> Files provided:
> train.csv — the 14,714 training records with feedback, one row per record (columns id, substrates,
> ec, candidate, label).
> test.csv — the 3,286 records to rank, one row per record (columns id, substrates, ec,
> candidate; the label is withheld and is what your relevance score orders).
> sample_submission.csv — an example submission in the exact required shape (columns id, score), one
> row per test.csv id. Its score values are a weak character-similarity baseline (the overlap
> between the candidate and substrate SMILES); it ranks only the easy negatives correctly and scores low
> (see Scoring). Replace each score with your own relevance.
> The columns present across the dataset, with their data type and meaning, are:
> id — type str. A unique record identifier, an opaque string (e.g. rxv-16tvhnh8x). Present in
> every file (train.csv, test.csv, sample_submission.csv, and your submission).
> substrates — type str (a dot-separated SMILES string). The enzyme-context's substrate molecules
> joined by . (e.g. CCO.O=O), including any cofactors. Present in train.csv and test.csv.
> ec — type str. The four-level Enzyme Commission number d.d.d.d of the catalyzing enzyme (leading
> digit = enzyme class 1–7). Together with substrates it defines the enzyme-context. Present in
> train.csv and test.csv.
> candidate — type str (a SMILES string). The candidate product molecule to score for relevance to
> the enzyme-context. Present in train.csv and test.csv.
> label — type int (0 or 1). The implicit relevance feedback for a training record: 1 if
> candidate is the enzyme-context's observed product, 0 if it is a sampled negative. It is the
> training signal you learn your ranker from — present in train.csv and withheld from test.csv
> (where your relevance score orders the records).
> Example train.csv rows (header + an observed-product and a sampled-negative record):
> id,substrates,ec,candidate,label rxv-16tvhnh8x,CCO.O=O,1.1.3.13,CC=O,1 rxv-9a1b2c3d4,CCO.O=O,1.1.1.1,CC=O,0
> (the second row is a hard negative: the same substrates and the same candidate aldehyde, but a different
> oxidoreductase ec from the same family that does not produce it under these conditions — so it should be
> ranked below the observed product.)
> The test candidates are drawn from a product-disjoint pool: every observed product molecule in the test
> set is absent from the training records, so you must rank enzyme-contexts and products you have never seen
> in training rather than memorize them.
> Submission format
> Submit a CSV named submission.csv with exactly two columns, id and score, one row per test record:
> Column	Type	Description
> id	str	A test record id; copy the id values verbatim from test.csv (each test id must appear exactly once).
> score	float	Your relevance score in [0, 1] for the record (how strongly the enzyme-context supports the candidate as its product). A missing or non-numeric value is treated as 0.5; values outside [0, 1] are clipped.
> Include the header row id,score and exactly one row per test id. The submission must have exactly these
> two columns, in that order (id then score) — any extra/missing column or different column order is
> rejected. Concrete example (submission.csv, header + three rows):
> id,score rxv-7c4e1a9d2,0.97 rxv-1b2c3d4e5,0.04 rxv-2c3d4e5f6,0.61
> Scoring
> This is a learning-to-rank problem: your relevance scores are judged on how well they order
> observed products above sampled negatives and how well-calibrated they are, blended into a single
> quality in [0, 1] and passed through a strict difficulty curve. Writing y_i in {0,1} for the relevance
> feedback of record i and score_i for your relevance score:
> 1. Ranking quality rank. The fraction of (observed-product, negative) record pairs your scores order
> correctly — the Mann–Whitney / ROC statistic, where 0.5 is a random ordering and 1 is a perfect one:
> rank = clip( 2 * AUC(score, y) - 1 , 0 , 1 )
> 2. Calibration cal (a log-loss proper scoring rule). With s_i your relevance clamped to
> [1e-9, 1-1e-9], the mean log-loss and its normalization against the uninformative constant-0.5 baseline
> ln 2:
> NLL = - mean over i of [ y_i * ln(s_i) + (1 - y_i) * ln(1 - s_i) ] cal = clip( 1 - NLL / ln(2) , 0 , 1 )
> cal = 1 for perfectly-calibrated relevance (s_i = y_i), cal = 0 for scoring 0.5 everywhere, and a
> confidently-wrong relevance (e.g. s = 0.99 on a negative) is penalized heavily — being sure and wrong
> costs you.
> 3. Hard-negative ranking hard_rank. A subset of the negatives is hard: an observed product paired
> with the wrong enzyme class from the same top-level EC family, so surface cues do not help. Restricting the
> ranking statistic to observed products versus these hard negatives:
> hard_rank = clip( 2 * AUC(score over {observed products} u {hard negatives}, y) - 1 , 0 , 1 )
> 4. Blend and difficulty shaping. The three parts are blended into a single quality, then mapped to the
> final score by a strict-but-smooth curve:
> quality = 0.45 * rank + 0.20 * cal + 0.35 * hard_rank score = 0.10 * quality + 0.25 * quality**2 + 0.65 * gate(quality)
> where gate(q) is a smooth "near-perfect" curve built from the hyperbolic tangent, normalized so
> gate(0) = 0 and gate(1) = 1:
> gate(q) = ( tanh( 300 * (q - 0.995) ) - tanh( 300 * (0 - 0.995) ) ) / ( tanh( 300 * (1 - 0.995) ) - tanh( 300 * (0 - 0.995) ) )
> What not to use
> This is a learning-to-rank / recommendation challenge. Each record pairs an enzyme-reaction context
> (substrates, ec) with a candidate product, and your solution outputs a single relevance score in
> [0, 1] for how strongly the enzyme-context supports that candidate as its product. Your solution must be a
> trained ranker / recommender that learns from the provided implicit relevance feedback (label 1 =
> observed product, 0 = sampled negative) and generalizes to the product-disjoint test set (whose observed
> product molecules never appear in training).
> Not allowed
> Retrieval / look-up of the feedback. Do not rank a record by looking the reaction up in any external
> reaction or enzyme database, the chemical literature, a web search / API, or a pretrained model's
> memorized reactions. The relevance must be modeled from the given record, not retrieved. (The test
> observed products are held out precisely to reward generalization, not recall.)
> A purely hand-written rule engine as the whole solution. A fixed table of "EC class X + functional
> group Y -> transformation Z" with no learned, calibrated component does not satisfy the intent.
> Hand-built rules are welcome as features or as a re-ranking / post-processing layer around a trained
> ranker.
> Any use of the test feedback or private files. Do not attempt to recover answers.csv, the withheld
> label/hard columns, or the true relevance for the test set by any means.
> id / metadata hacking. The id is an opaque token; it encodes nothing about the relevance. Do not
> attempt to infer relevance from ids, row order, feedback-balance counting, or file statistics.
> Degenerate constant scores. Scoring 0.5 (or any constant) on every record scores 0 by construction —
> the score rewards ordering observed products above negatives and calibrated relevance, not a lucky
> threshold. Hard-coding outputs or otherwise gaming the grader rather than modeling the chemistry is
> disallowed.

Full challenge description from page:

> Overview:
>
> A nineteenth-century bookseller kept a credit account for every returning customer: a page per person, headed with their occupational title and place of residence, and every purchase entered underneath as it happened. This challenge asks whether the rest of a customer's reading can be recovered from almost nothing.
>
> Each case is one customer observed up to a chronological cut-off. You are given the occupational title recorded at the head of their account, their place of residence, and the first few works they bought, in purchase order. You are then given a pool of two hundred candidate works drawn from the shop's catalogue. Some of those two hundred are works this customer really did buy later in the decade. The rest are decoys. Your task is to rank the pool so the works the customer actually bought come first.
>
> The same customer appears at several cut-offs, after their second, third, fourth and fifth purchase, so a model can be trained on how a reading profile sharpens as evidence accumulates. Every case belonging to one customer sits entirely within one split: a customer seen at any cut-off in training never appears in the evaluation set at any cut-off.
>
> Every customer in the evaluation set is one you have never seen. You cannot look them up, and you cannot learn their taste from their own history, because a handful of purchases is all the history there is. What you can learn is how occupation, residence and an opening handful of purchases relate to everything else a person of that kind carried out of the shop.
>
> The decoys are the difficult part. They are not random works. Each decoy is drawn to match the recorded-sales count of one of the works the customer really bought, as closely as the catalogue allows: 80.3 percent of decoys match a true work's sales count exactly and 88.9 percent match within a single sale. Popularity has been engineered out of the candidate pool on purpose, and the global sales count is never published. What is left is the relationship between a reader and a book.
>
> Relation to prior work: Both the underlying data and the nearest prior competition are attributed here, and the differences in task design are stated precisely.
>
> Underlying data. The records are the transcribed credit sales ledgers of Gumpert's bookshop, Gothenburg, deposited by Henning Hansen at DataverseNO under DOI 10.18710/HWLJYL and released under CC0 1.0. The ledgers are a historical record and are not claimed as original work here; the contribution of this challenge is the prediction task constructed on them, not the transcription.
>
> Nearest prior competition. A Kaggle class competition titled "Future purchases from book seller" (kaggle.com/c/msiabook) ran from January 2018 on the general problem of predicting a bookseller's future purchases. It cannot share data with this challenge: the ledger transcription used here was generated on 27 December 2021 and first published on 13 January 2022, four years after that competition opened. Any resemblance is at the level of the generic phrase "predict what a bookshop customer buys next", which is a task family, not a benchmark.
>
> What is specific to this task design:
>
> Split. Cold start by customer, with every case of a customer held together. Forty percent of cases are held out, and an evaluation customer appears nowhere in training at any cut-off, so nothing can be memorised per customer. Generalisation must come from other customers.
> Chronological cut-offs. Each customer yields a case after their 2nd, 3rd, 4th and 5th purchase where their history is long enough, giving 1,436 cases from 492 customer accounts. The evidence available to the model grows across cut-offs while the customer stays fixed.
> Candidate-decoy selection. Each case is scored against a bounded pool of exactly 200 works, and every decoy is drawn by matching the global recorded-sales count of one of the true works, nearest count first: 80.3 percent match exactly, 88.9 percent within one sale, and the remainder are the closest counts the catalogue still had free. The sales count is then withheld from the published catalogue. The effect is measured below: ranking by popularity scores 0.045 where a perfect ranking scores 1.0.
> Evaluation. MAP@20 over a 200-work pool, with the cut-off chosen from the data rather than by convention: 98.4 percent of cases have 20 or fewer relevant works, so 20 is the smallest cut that scores almost every case at its full ceiling. The floor and ceiling are measured, not asserted.
> Privacy and domain constraint. Customer names and ledger page numbers are both removed from the published data and replaced with a single opaque hashed account identifier that is not reversible to either. The ledger records real, named nineteenth-century individuals, and re-identifying them is out of scope and prohibited. Two further fields are deterministically withheld to prevent shortcutting: the occupational title is absent for about 40 percent of accounts (a 35 percent deterministic redaction on top of the accounts the ledger already left blank) and the imprint year for about 68 percent of catalogue works (a 30 percent redaction on top of what the ledger omitted).
>
> Evaluation: The metric is mean average precision at 20, written MAP@20.
>
> For one case, take your ranked list, keep the first 20 entries, and walk down it. Each time you hit a work the customer really bought, record the precision of the list up to that point. Average those values and divide by the number of relevant works, or by 20 if there are more than 20. That is the average precision for the case. The score for the challenge is the mean of that quantity over every case in the evaluation set. Higher is better; the range is 0 to 1.
>
> Why this metric: each case has several relevant works in the pool rather than one, so a metric built for a single correct answer would throw away most of the signal. The number of relevant works varies from 3 to 25 across cases, so a fixed-size set metric would be unfair in both directions: it would be impossible to score well on a case with 25 relevant works and trivially easy on one with 3. Average precision handles both because it normalises by the number of relevant items. It is also rank-sensitive throughout the list, which matters here because the interesting difference between a weak and a strong solution is whether the true purchases land in the top handful or merely somewhere in the top twenty.
>
> The cut-off is 20 rather than 200 because 98.4 percent of cases have 20 or fewer relevant works, so a list of 20 is long enough for almost every case to be scored at its full ceiling, while still forcing you to commit to an ordering instead of returning the whole pool. No threshold is applied anywhere, because nothing in this task requires a calibrated probability - only an order.
>
> No second component is combined with average precision, and this is deliberate. A set-overlap term such as F1 at a fixed cut-off would be redundant, because with a single ranked list per case and a single notion of relevance, average precision already determines the quality of that list completely. It would also be gameable independently of the ranking: average precision depends only on the order the scores induce, while a fixed threshold depends on their absolute values, so a solver could leave its ranking untouched and move a threshold-based term by rescaling. A macro-average over cases would also be redundant, because every case contributes exactly one list and one average precision value, so the plain mean already weights every case equally.
>
> Floor and ceiling, all measured on the held-out cases. A perfect submission scores 1.0. Ranking each pool at random scores 0.022, and the supplied sample_submission scores 0.020. Ranking every pool by how often each work was bought by the training customers - the strongest shortcut that ignores the customer entirely - scores 0.045, which is what the frequency-matched decoy construction is for. Ranking by occupational affinity to genre alone scores 0.025. A frozen multilingual sentence encoder, used inference-only to rank each pool by cosine similarity between the case profile and the work text, scores 0.035: off-the-shelf semantic similarity is worse than popularity here, because the answer is not "works that resemble your seed purchases". A simple collaborative baseline that scores each candidate by how often it was bought together with the seed works in the training accounts reaches 0.074, and combining it with occupational affinity reaches 0.081. These numbers are what the min and max in config.yaml are set against.
>
> Dataset: The following files are provided in public/.
>
> train.csv - one row per training case, 861 rows. case_id - text - identifier for this customer-and-cut-off case. profession - text - the occupational title at the head of the account, in Swedish. Empty for roughly 35 percent of accounts, where the ledger head is missing, illegible, or deliberately withheld. place - text - the recorded place of residence or workplace. Empty where not recorded. seed_work_ids - text - the works bought by this customer up to the cut-off, as space-separated work_id values in purchase order. candidate_work_ids - text - the pool of 200 candidate work_id values for this case, space-separated, in randomised order.
>
> train_labels.csv - the training labels, one row per training case, 861 rows, in the same order as train.csv. Join it to train.csv on case_id. case_id - text - identifier matching train.csv. purchased_work_ids - text - the works in that case's candidate pool that the customer really bought after the cut-off, space-separated. This is the training target.
>
> test.csv - one row per evaluation case, 575 rows, with exactly the same feature columns as train.csv in the same order. These customers appear nowhere in train.csv at any cut-off, and no label file is provided for them.
>
> catalogue.csv - one row per work that appears in any candidate pool, 1,996 rows. work_id - text - stable identifier for the work; this is the value you rank and submit. work_title - text - the title as written in the ledger, abbreviated and inconsistently spelled. author - text - the author as written, usually surname only. Empty for about 40 percent of works. genre - text - a genre abbreviation from a 20-category scheme; unid marks an unidentified work. language - text - a language abbreviation across 7 values. Empty means Swedish. imprint_year - text - the edition year where recorded. Empty for about 68 percent of works, partly because the ledger omitted it and partly because it has been withheld here.
>
> sample_submission.csv - a correctly formatted submission with the required columns and row order, scoring 0.020.
>
> Submission: Submit a CSV with exactly these two columns, named exactly as shown. A submission whose columns are renamed is rejected rather than scored.
>
> case_id - text - one row for each case_id in test.csv, 575 rows plus a header row. ranked_work_ids - text - your ranked candidate work_id values for that case, space-separated, best first. Only the first 20 entries are scored. Every id you submit should come from that case's own candidate_work_ids pool; ids from outside the pool are never correct.
>
> Example, with fabricated identifiers for illustration:
>
> case_id,ranked_work_ids case_0001example,w1a2b3c4d5 w9f8e7d6c5 w4b5c6d7e8 w7c8d9e0f1 case_0002example,w9f8e7d6c5 w2d3e4f5a6 w6e7f8a9b0 case_0003example,w3e4f5a6b7 w8a9b0c1d2 w1a2b3c4d5 w5f6a7b8c9
>
> Requirements: a header row, one row per test case, in any order. Write the file without a pandas index column. A missing or empty ranked_work_ids cell is scored zero for that case rather than rejected, and a duplicated case_id is rejected.
>
> Rules: The only valid input signal is the case attributes and seed purchases given in test.csv, together with the work metadata in catalogue.csv and whatever you learn from train.csv and train_labels.csv.
>
> The following approaches are not allowed:
>
> Hardcoding predictions for specific test cases.
> Using the case_id string itself as a prediction signal. It is an opaque hash provided so you can join rows, not so you can mine it.
> Using the position of a work inside the candidate_work_ids string, the row order of test.csv, or the row order of catalogue.csv as a prediction signal. Pool order is randomised per case and carries no information about relevance.
> Downloading the source ledger transcription, or any mirror or derivative of it, and using it to recover the withheld purchases. The source is openly attributed above for provenance, and it is openly licensed, so this is easy to do and it is the one exploit that would completely defeat the challenge. The evaluation labels are real historical facts copied from that deposit, so fetching it is retrieval of the answer key rather than modelling. Attribution is not permission to consult the source: everything you need is in public/.
> Attempting to re-identify the real nineteenth-century individuals behind the opaque account identifiers, whether from the source deposit, genealogical records, or any other archive.
> Using any other external corpus, catalogue or bibliographic database to reconstruct which customer bought which work.
> Using private, role-gated, or API-key-based models, or calling any external inference API at inference time.
> Using non-reproducible external weights or artifacts not publicly available.
>
> The intended task is to learn how a reader's recorded social position and opening purchases predict the rest of their reading, from 861 training cases and the works themselves. The exploits listed above bypass exactly that: pool order and row order would let a solution score without ever representing a customer, and an external lookup of the source ledger would return the labels directly. What a real solution should capture is that an occupational title constrains genre and language, that a place of residence carries information about a customer's milieu, that the opening purchases locate a reader inside the shop's clientele, and that works bought together by similar customers go on being bought together.
>
> Pretrained model policy: Fine-tuning a publicly available pretrained model is allowed and is the intended route. A multilingual text encoder is a reasonable starting point, because the titles, abbreviated author names and Swedish occupational titles are short, noisy and span Swedish, German, English, French and Danish. Measure the frozen baseline before relying on one: a frozen multilingual sentence encoder, used inference-only to rank each pool by cosine similarity, scores 0.035 MAP@20, which is below the 0.045 popularity shortcut and well below the 0.081 collaborative baseline. Any fine-tuned system you submit should beat the frozen baseline it starts from, otherwise the encoder is doing nothing that the pool construction has not already defeated. The training signal available for that fine-tuning is 861 cases times 200 candidates, or 172,200 labelled case-candidate pairs, which is what makes a GPU-scale cross-encoder or bi-encoder the intended competitive route rather than an optional one. Nothing in this challenge is solvable by a pretrained model alone, because the target is not a property of the works but of which nineteenth-century individual bought them.

Full challenge description from page:

> Music Context Preference Ranking Overview This is a Recommendation-lane challenge about personalized music ranking. The prediction unit is a listener-to-song candidate, not an independent row prediction: each hidden query is one listener choosing among a slate of candidate songs. Assign a numeric score to every candidate so that the strongest songs appear first within that listener's slate. The task evaluates familiar-listener recommendation for unseen catalog items. Listener personality measurements recur across labeled and hidden queries, but participant identity, song identity, and the test grouping key are not exposed. The split holds out complete songs and their audio vectors, so a model must transfer from the public listener and song measurements instead of recovering a song-level rating lookup. The feature files are kept schema-identical; public training labels are supplied separately in train_targets.csv, keyed by the training id. The hidden responses capture three related recommendation decisions: immediate appeal, willingness to listen to similar songs in the future, and willingness to recommend the song to a friend. Training exposes only their equal-weight aggregate relevance label, while the private evaluator checks whether the within-slate ranking remains useful across all three contexts. The CSV files are only the transport format for this grouped recommendation problem. Dataset File descriptions train.csv -- 3,866 candidate rows from 259 listener groups containing the 49 public personality and audio measurements. Listener-group membership and labels are not included in this feature file. train_targets.csv -- 3,866 public training labels keyed by the id values in train.csv. Its target column is the aggregate relevance label used for supervised training. test.csv -- 854 unlabeled candidate rows from 87 hidden listener groups and 185 held-out songs. It contains the same 49 model features as train.csv but does not contain target or hidden listener-group membership. sample_submission.csv -- A valid template with one row for every test id, filled with deterministic nonzero random ranking scores. Column descriptions id (string) -- Opaque candidate-row identifier. Copy each test value exactly once into the submission. personality_01 through personality_20 (float) -- Anonymous listener personality-domain and facet measurements. These values repeat within a listener group because they describe the listener rather than the song. audio_01 through audio_29 (float) -- Anonymous low-level acoustic and music-emotion measurements for the candidate song. target (float, in train_targets.csv) -- Aggregate training relevance in 0, 1], computed as ((Q1 + Q2 + Q3) - 3) / 12 from the three hidden response axes. Evaluation Submissions are scored with context_balanced_ndcg_at_5. Higher is better. The private evaluator keeps the three original 1--5 response axes for each test row, converts each response to relevance response - 1, and evaluates the submitted ranking separately for every hidden query_id. > The Challenge A user's behavior is never observed in a vacuum. What they watch, skip, like, or ignore depends partly on what they currently want and partly on what the recommendation system decided to place in front of them. This challenge asks models to separate those two effects well enough to recognize the same short-term preference state through two different exposure mechanisms. Each row contains a sparse sequence of randomly exposed videos and eight richer behavioral windows produced by the ordinary recommendation policy. All eight candidate windows belong to the same user. One of them comes from the same hidden period as the random-exposure sequence. Your task is to identify that window. The clock is deliberately unavailable. Calendar dates, absolute timestamps, source sequence positions, persistent user identifiers, and persistent video identifiers are removed before release. Candidate windows are also selected to resemble one another in coarse activity and engagement statistics. The problem is therefore not user matching, timestamp recovery, or simple engagement-profile similarity. It is an eight-way sequence-matching problem in which the model must decide which policy-selected behavioral state is compatible with a sparse randomized probe of the same evolving user. For every sample, submit one code from C01 through C08. Why the Two Sides Look Different The two sequences in an episode come from different observation processes. The probe trace is small. It contains only a handful of randomly exposed videos, but those exposures are less shaped by the serving recommender. The candidate windows are much denser. They show dozens of ordinary recommendation events and therefore reveal more about what the user actually did over a short period. At the same time, the candidate sequence is filtered through the serving policy, which strongly influences which items were available to react to. This asymmetry is intentional. A useful representation should be able to compare a sparse, weakly policy-selected probe with a dense, strongly policy-selected behavioral state without pretending that the two streams were generated in the same way. The benchmark does not ask for next-item prediction, click-through-rate estimation, propensity scores, uplift, inverse-propensity weighting, or causal treatment effects. The only target is temporal correspondence between two policy-conditioned views of the same latent preference state. What an Episode Contains The public files contain the following columns: sample_id development_fold user_context probe_trace candidate_states Training data additionally contains: state_id The structured fields are JSON encoded inside the CSV. No images are used. user_context contains a compact set of user attributes that do not depend on the episode timestamp. These can include activity-degree category, low-activity-period status, live-streamer status, video-author status, follower and fan buckets, friend-count bucket, and registration-age bucket. The user context is shared by all eight candidates in the row. It can help the model interpret behavior, but it cannot identify the correct candidate. probe_trace contains an ordered random-exposure sequence, typically 4 to 12 events. Public event features may include relative position, coarse relative time-gap bucket, scenario token, video type, upload type, duration bucket, aspect-ratio bucket, music-type token, tag tokens, click, like, follow, comment, forward, hate, long-view, profile-entry, watch-ratio bucket, and strong-feedback count. candidate_states contains exactly eight standard-policy windows. Each candidate typically contains 64 to 128 events represented with the same public event vocabulary. All eight candidates belong to the user represented by the probe trace. The candidate labels are permuted independently for every sample. C04 in one row has no connection to C04 in another. Exactly one candidate is the hidden temporal match. How the Hidden Match Is Defined Episodes are built only from the period in which randomized and ordinary recommendation exposures coexist. A short group of nearby random-exposure events is first selected for one user. The private midpoint of that group defines the hidden anchor interval. The gold candidate is a standard-policy behavioral window centered on that same hidden interval. Seven additional standard-policy windows are drawn from other moments in the same user's history. These decoys are required to come from sufficiently separated intervals so that they represent distinct local states rather than tiny shifts of the same sequence. The original anchor time is never released. Neither the participant nor the grader asks the model to reconstruct the timestamp. The timestamp exists only to determine which candidate is correct during preparation. This design makes all eight candidates equally valid descriptions of the same person while only one corresponds to the same moment in that person's evolving behavior. The Time-Blindness Rules Preparation removes direct chronological shortcuts before a row becomes public. The released episode contains no: absolute timestamp calendar date hour of day original user sequence index persistent user ID persistent video ID source log name source row original candidate chronology Every candidate sequence is locally reindexed from zero. The probe is reindexed independently. A participant cannot tell whether C01 occurred before C07, how many hours separated them, or whether either was close to the probe by reading a public timestamp. Relative event spacing may still appear in coarse logarithmic buckets because local temporal dynamics are part of the intended sequence signal. The benchmark removes global synchronization cues, not within-window temporal structure. Preventing Easy Item Matching Persistent item identity is also removed. A random probe can occasionally contain a video that also appears in ordinary recommendation history. Video type, upload type, music type, duration, aspect ratio, and tag information are useful precisely because they describe what kind of content produced a response rather than exposing which exact source item it was. This leaves the model with semantic and behavioral compatibility rather than an equality join. Decoys Are Chosen Deliberately The seven incorrect candidates are not random slices of history. For each episode, preparation builds a larger pool of same-user windows and searches for decoys that resemble the gold window under cheap summary statistics. Matching can use event-count bands, relative-gap distributions, tab composition, click rate, long-view rate, watch-ratio bands, strong-feedback density, duration distributions, and repeated-item-rate bands before persistent item IDs are removed. A candidate set is discarded when the gold window is a conspicuous outlier. Individual events carry semantic information about the exposed item and feedback information about the user's response.
